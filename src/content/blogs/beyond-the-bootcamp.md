# I Wasn't Selected. Here's What My PixelCompute Journey Taught Me.

*An honest account of deadlines, late-night stand-ups, JavaScript, coding under pressure, and learning more about myself than I expected.*

Opportunities in college often come with a certain kind of excitement. You apply, wait for the results, and hope to make it through the next round. But sometimes, the most valuable part of an opportunity isn't where you end up. It's everything that happens along the way.

My PixelCompute journey was one such experience.

It started with campus placements. The selection process had around three to four stages, and what initially seemed like another opportunity to test my coding skills turned into an experience that challenged me in ways I hadn't anticipated.

## From thousands of applicants to a training bootcamp

The initial selection process was quite competitive. Out of approximately 2,000–3,000 students, around 500 were shortlisted for the second online assessment.

I was genuinely happy when I found out that I had made it to the next round. Eventually, after the second round, only 94 students were selected for the Stage 2 training program.

Being among those selected felt exciting. It was an opportunity to learn, improve my technical skills, and experience something beyond the usual college routine.

But getting shortlisted was only the beginning.

After about a week of the training program, nearly 20 students were removed from the process, leaving approximately 74 participants.

That's when I began to understand that this wasn't simply about getting selected and attending a few sessions. We had to consistently demonstrate our progress.

## The 10 PM routine

One of the most distinctive parts of the experience was the daily routine.

We were divided into batches, with each batch assigned a mentor. Every day, we had to update our completed work on Slack, and at 10 PM, we would join our daily stand-up call.

These weren't just attendance meetings.

During the stand-ups, mentors would ask questions about what we had learned. We were sometimes asked to share our screens, explain our code, and work through coding questions. It wasn't enough to simply say that a task was completed. We had to understand what we had written and be able to explain it.

Alongside these sessions, we had deadlines to complete JavaScript modules on the BigBinary Academy website.

This meant that learning, completing modules, updating progress, and preparing to explain our work became part of the daily routine.

Our mentor was genuinely supportive. Whenever we had doubts, they were willing to clarify them, and we could reach out when we needed permission or assistance. Having someone approachable throughout the process made the experience considerably better.

## Learning while keeping up

The technical learning was one of the most valuable parts of the bootcamp.

I spent time learning JavaScript through the BigBinary Academy modules. It gave me an opportunity to explore a language that I was actively preparing to work with and understand concepts through practical exercises.

But the challenge wasn't limited to learning something new.

I also had my regular college responsibilities to manage: classes, assignments, quizzes, and other academic commitments. Trying to keep up with both college and the bootcamp wasn't always easy.

There were days when I couldn't complete the assigned tasks in time. Sometimes, I had to take a leave because I simply couldn't manage everything together.

It was a reminder that participating in an intensive training program while being a college student requires more than just technical ability. It requires planning, consistency, and time management.

And honestly, I was still figuring those things out.

## The pressure of showing your work

One thing I hadn't fully anticipated before joining was the importance of presenting your work every day.

Writing code and explaining code are two different things.

When you're working independently, you have the freedom to experiment, make mistakes, search for solutions, and take your time understanding a problem. But during a stand-up, you have to be prepared to explain your approach, answer questions, and sometimes solve something in front of others.

Initially, this could feel intimidating.

However, something gradually changed. I became less afraid of answering questions. I started feeling more comfortable speaking up, explaining my understanding, and presenting my work.

I consider this one of my biggest personal takeaways from the experience.

As engineering students, many of us carry a certain fear of interviews: What if I don't know the answer? What if I get stuck? What if someone asks me to explain something I have written?

This experience helped me realise that answering questions is a skill that improves with practice. You don't always need to know everything; sometimes, being willing to think aloud and communicate your understanding matters just as much.

## The assessment that changed things

And then came another online assessment for the next stage.

This was where my journey came to an end.

I couldn't clear the assessment, and I wasn't selected to move forward.

Looking back, I think the biggest challenge wasn't necessarily the questions themselves. It was how I approached the time available to solve them.

During much of the assessment, I found myself worrying about the clock. I was conscious of how quickly the time was passing, and that pressure affected the way I approached the questions.

Instead of concentrating entirely on breaking down the problems and finding solutions, I was also constantly thinking about how little time remained.

That's something I hadn't properly prepared for.

I had made progress in overcoming my fear of answering questions and presenting my work, but solving coding problems under strict time constraints was a different challenge altogether.

I realised that knowing a concept and being able to apply it efficiently during a timed assessment are two separate things.

And that's an area I need to work on.

## What I took away from the experience

Not getting selected was disappointing, of course. But it also gave me a clearer understanding of what I need to improve.

I learned JavaScript, became more comfortable presenting my work, and experienced a structured training environment with daily accountability.

More importantly, I understood that technical preparation isn't just about learning concepts or completing modules. It also involves being able to manage time, think clearly under pressure, and apply what you've learned when it matters.

If I could go through the experience again, I would pay much more attention to timed problem-solving practice. I would work on managing the clock without allowing it to distract me from the actual problem.

I would also plan my college and training commitments more carefully so that I could maintain consistency without constantly feeling overwhelmed.

## It wasn't the outcome I wanted, but it was an experience worth having.

I entered PixelCompute hoping to move forward through the selection process. I didn't reach the final outcome I wanted, but I did leave with a better understanding of my strengths and the areas I need to improve.

Sometimes, an experience doesn't give you the result you were hoping for. Instead, it shows you what you should work on next.

For me, that means continuing to learn, practising under timed conditions, and becoming more confident not just in explaining code, but in solving problems when the clock is ticking.

And perhaps that's what I'll remember most about this journey.
